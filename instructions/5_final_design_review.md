# General Description

- The Final Design Review (FDR) is the phase in the design process where you review in detail all of the design work that has been done throughout the process to assess expected capabilities and requirements of the final product. This is intended to help identify and correct any last minute mistakes or errors before the final product gets sent to manufacturing, and check if any further improvement is still needed. Similar to the CDR, it is typically attended by other engineers, managers, investors, and customers who evaluate the suitability of the final design in greater detail. The FDR involves thorough examination of each subsystem as well as complete system integration in detail, to provide an overall evaluation on the capabilities of the product and a decision on whether this product can be relased to the market (or should go back to the design cycle).

- In this class, we are using the FDR to formally demonstrate that you have developed a fully functional design that meets your expected product requirements---we will be using the FDR as an opportunity to identify and validate the engineered capabilities of your final product design, with support from related works, mathematical and modeling analysis, simulations, and experimentation. As part of this FDR of your product, you will be required to give a comprehensive oral presentation, a physical interactive demonstration, and a final written report, along with a product pitch and executive summary, due over the last two weeks of class.

- Final demonstrations will be held at a public demo day, one of Mon or Fri, Week 11, 8-11am.

# Forms and Due Dates

## Elevator Pitch

- **Content**– The elevator pitch will include 3-5 minutes of highlights of your final product design, focusing primarily on the connection between your motivating problem and engineering outcomes.  Use technical content sparingly to provide support as needed.

- **Form**– Recorded, 3 to 5 minutes.

- **Due date**– \pitchdue, uploaded to Youtube or some other video hosting service, and linked to in the Bruinlearn forums.

- **Note 1**– As with any presentation, take advantage of the media being using.

- **Note 2**– This is the **only** deliverable that primarily focuses on the overall **product** itself as opposed to its subsystem **design decisions**.

- **Note 3**– This elevator pitch is your opportunity to sell your clients / customers on your future product---consider this to be a commercial / advertisement targetting them directly. Make use of this opportunity to raise the final round valuation by **demonstrating** how your solution generates value to those stakeholders.

## Comprehensive Oral Presentation

- **Content**– The oral presentation will communicate **all** of your engineered solutions along with their validation, to a level of detail according to its value to the final product design.

- **Form**– In-class presentation, 20-25 minutes: 1-2 minutes introduction/brief project definition recap, 10-15 minutes technical content, 3-5 minutes description of overall design outcome versus (updated) problem requirements, and 3-5 minutes conclusion and discussion.

- **Involvement**– All team members are expected to speak/present on the work they have done (~equal time each). A team leader or any combination of team members can present the introduction/brief project summary/outcome comparison/conclusion.

- **Due date**– Slides due \presentationdue on Bruinlearn.

- **Note 1**– This oral presentation is **instead of** the weekly deliverables update presentation you would normally present at that time (you do NOT need to submit those slides).

- **Note 2**– Take advantage of the media we are using.  As always, static text, images, or screenshots are of particularly low value.  Rather, employ live demos of physical artifacts, code execution, running CAD and/or simulations to demonstrate and communicate the functionality of what you have developed.

- **Note 3**– The "conclusion" is **not** simply a summary; it is **not** using the word in the sense of an ending.  It is used to draw inferences: what can the stakeholders (users, clients, other engineers, managers, etc.) conclude (i.e. derive) about the impact of your engineering efforts on your defined problem statement / motivation?

## Executive summary

- **Content**- As a part of your Final Design Review (FDR), you will typically prepare an executive summary that gives a brief, high level overview that summarizes the key points of your design process to date and sets expectations for the complete review.  In this class, we will frame this executive summary within the context of the ABET requirements.

- **Form**– Written report consisting of three labeled subsections; strict 2 page limit total

- **Due date**– \summarydue, submitted on Bruinlearn

- **Note 1**– You will be presenting only your final claims, leaving the support for the full FDR report.  As such, you should have minimal data that you have not generated yourself, and so you should similarly have very few citations / references.

## Written Final Report

- **Content**– The written report will include all relevant content on the work you have completed on your project over the past two quarters, collected and organized clearly in order to best communicate the content. The length of the written report is not specified, but it should thoroughly document everything you have done.

- **Form**– Written report, Unlimited number of pages

- **Due date**– \paperdue submitted on Bruinlearn

- **Note 1**– "Everything you have done" includes pivots or dead ends, rejected candidate subsystems, or other engineering efforts that may not otherwise be visible in the final design presentation or demo.

- **Note 2**– Effectiveness of communication is a priority in this document---avoid having this be a raw data dump.  The outline provided below might be a good starting point for organization, but feel free to adapt as necessary to maximize clarity.  The idea is for all stakeholders to have no remanining unanswered questions after reading your document---their conclusions about your final design should match yours.

# Executive summary content

-  *An ability to communicate effectively with a range of audiences*

Note that the audience for this document consists of all of your stakeholders, and so you should use the appropriate terminology and framing.

This document must contain the following three subsections exactly.

## Requirements

-  *An ability to apply engineering design to produce solutions that meet specified needs with consideration of public health, safety, and welfare, as well as global, cultural, social, environmental, and economic factors*

Summarize your final problem specification (after it has evolved over the course of your design process).  Be sure to identify your stakeholders; capture the pain point targetted by your final design, its root cause(s) that your solution addresses, and the quantified value you expect your solution will generate.

Also include your analysis of the noteworthy negative externalities associated with the adoption of your designed product.  Externalities refer to broader consequences within the world; these must be effects explicitly on **non-**stakeholders.

## System design

-  *An ability to acquire and apply new knowledge as needed, using appropriate learning strategies*

Summarize your final system design as it has been refined over the course of your design process.  Identify the technical design decisions you have resolved and the subsystem dependencies (i.e. requirements and constraints) those decisions have satisfied.  Follow the dependencies back to a system level quantification of costs and benefits to the stakeholders.  This will likely take the form of a table or (nested) bullet point list.

## Project plan

-  *An ability to function effectively on a team whose members together provide leadership, create a collaborative and inclusive environment, establish goals, plan tasks, and meet objectives*

Summarize a postmortem of your project plan, comparing your originally planned milestones from your PDR to the actual execution of your design process.  Note both successes as well as modified or missed objectives, and use that to place your FDR within the product design/development cycle.

# Report Content

## Part 1: Project Definition (from PDR)

The first portion of the FDR is to define the project. This was largely accomplished in the PDR, and content can be copied/adapted from the PDR where appropriate. This section should define the problem, the scope of the solution being considered, the requirements/specifications of the solution, and the process by which a solution was selected to solve the problem given the requirements/specifications. Any background information needed to understand the problem and/or solution should be presented.

## Part 2: Design/Engineering Work

 The second portion of your FDR is to document all the engineering work you have accomplished since the PDR. This includes summaries of the deliverables relevant to demonstrating the capabilities of your design (at the subsystem as well as integrated system level) as have been specified in your PDR and deliverables lists. **In addition to what you have done, be sure to detail why you did it the way you did (why you made the design choices you did, what and why alternatives were rejected, and what quantitative support you have for that decision), and how the deliverable has adequately demonstrated the capability of your design.** You should particularly describe any design decisions that resulted in deviations from the solution presented in the PDR---what information justified the change made, how did you obtain said information, and how did you verify it. It should be clear in both the written and oral report which team member(s) were responsible the tasks that lead to each deliverable.

## Part 3: Design Outcome versus Requirements – Comparison

This section of your FDR is linked to self-evaluation on the success of your integrated design outcome. You should describe in detail whether/how your final product meets problem capabilities and requirements. Specifically, you should list all of the capabilities and requirements presented in the PDR which you wanted your product to meet as driven by the motivating problem, and address each item by describing how well your integrated system design outcome satisfies it. Especially if you have refined your scope and capabilities in the CDR, there may be remaining unkowns/concerns your project would need to answer---if the current state of your design is not able to demonstrate some required capabilities, elaborate on that shortfall and explain what further actions would need to be taken to overcome those limitations.
