# General description (Framework)

## Notes

1. Top Down Approach and the Design Process – The formal design process follows a top-down approach. As a result the lecture and the reference reading materials only address this approach with three technology alternatives for a single core driver.

2. Specificity of Problem Definition –  Your core driver must be specifically defined. In the case of top-down, this means you have identified a single problem precisely enough to list the requirements such that you can evaluate how well the capabilities of the three considered technologies/solutions would meet the requirements. For example, “Assisting the Disabled” is NOT sufficiently defined: it is actually a field by itself including an enormous number of problems and it doesn’t tell us what the system needs to accomplish or how we will evaluate success; there are countless ways to “Assist the Disabled”. One would need to define if the product is an assistive technology or rehabilitation technology. This decision should be followed by the specific neuromuscular or other disorder that your potential customer may be suffering. Within such a disorder the designer is expected to define the specific mode of assistance or rehabilitation therapy that will be offered or administered. A detailed description of a problem may be described as follows:

    Individuals with advanced Parkinson’s Disease (10+ years from onset) generally suffer from tremors/shaking that prevents them from performing activities of daily living. Among other tasks, the shaking makes it difficult for the individuals to feed themselves as food will vibrate off eating utensils. The problem we aim to solve is enabling an individual with Parkinson’s to eat without spilling. The problem is constrained to an at-home environment, eating from a standard dinner plate (approximately 12 inch diameter), and the food consumed will be fried rice (as a common, easily spillable food). Further, the tremors of the Parkinson’s patient are identified to be at 2Hz, maximum translation due to tremors is constrained to 3 inches, and maximum orientation change due to tremor is constrained to 30 degrees (note, these are anecdotal constraints based off observations of a patient with Parkinsons, you will need to provide justification and/or references for any assumptions/constraints you make). Success will be measured by the volume of food spilled (as a % of the volume consumed) and time required to consume the food (normalized by the volume consumed) compared with and without the solution. Note, as details have been given for this example application/problem, no team will be allowed to use it.

3. Specificity of Solution -  Based on the problem definition, we have a specific task that we can propose solutions for, and requirements to evaluate the capabilities of the proposed solution against. Three solutions might be:
    1. A robot arm that uses computer vision to manipulate a standard fork to pick up rice and insert it in the individual’s mouth when a button is pressed.
    2. A modified spork with actuation elements such that the end of the spork remains steady despite the tremors of the hand holding it.
    3. A wrist exoskeleton using EMG to detect contraction of muscles in the forearm, and apply counter torques to suppress tremors of the hand.

    For each of these solutions (which all solve the same problem in similar but unique ways), the capabilities of the proposed systems to fulfill the problem requirements would need to be estimated (based on justifications and/or references to the literature) such that they could be compared against each other.

4. Bottom Up Approach - The above example is for a top-down approach. A similar level of specificity is required for the bottom-up. The technology must be defined specifically enough that it’s capabilities can be listed. Three applications must be proposed that are specific enough for the requirements to be listed, and are unique but closely related. Then, you can compare the extent to which the capabilities of the technology will likely satisfy the specific requirements of each of the applications.

5. Uniqueness - Your arguments, plans, and slides should be specific. If any of your content can be copy-pasted into another team’s presentation with little issue, it is not specific enough, and probably does not add value to your deliverable.

6. Feasibility & Scope - Many of the ideas presented for P2 were too grand in scope. Please keep in mind that you are a team of 3-4 senior engineering students with other obligations related to your studies and life. Furthermore, you will have approximately 16 weeks after this to solve your problem. When defining your problem/solution, look for the effort required to complete related projects. If your idea was most closely accomplished by a research lab of 10 graduate students and postdoctoral researchers over the course of 5 years or a Fortune 500 company in 2 years, your idea is likely 10-1000 times too large in scope.

#### Define Negative Externalities

All application-technology (or problem-solution) pairs have direct effects (solving the problem), as well as side effects. Automation generally impacts jobs, manufacturing has environmental impacts, and nearly every new solution/practice will disrupt an old solution/practice. Outcomes may be only accessible to a subset of the population, which will have an impact on the remainder.  For each alternative (whether bottom-up or top-down) in its own slide, present some of the major negative externalities (broader consequences to the world) associated with applying the technology to the application. Then, apply at least one ethical framework (e.g. deontology, consequentialism, etc) to justify why your application-technology pairing is ethically responsible to implement despite the externalities.
